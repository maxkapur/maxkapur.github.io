A handy class of submodular functions

Posted January 1, 2024

In this post, we will show that functions of the form

f(X)=1−∏i∈Ω∖X(1−pi)∏i∈X(1−qi) f(X) = 1 - \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} (1 - p_i) \prod_{i \in X} (1 - q_i)

are submodular for pi,qi∈[0,1]p_i, q_i \in [0, 1] where each pi≤qi,p_i \leq q_i, and examine an application of this small result that demonstrates its practical value.

Background on submodular functions

Given a ground set Ω,\Omega, a function f:2Ω↦Rf: 2^\Omega \mapsto \mathbb{R} is called submodular if, for any X⊆Y⊆ΩX \subseteq Y \subseteq \Omega and j∈Ω∖Y,j \in \Omega \setminus Y, we have

f(X∪{j})−f(X)≥f(Y∪{j})−f(Y). f(X \cup \{j\}) - f(X) \geq f(Y \cup \{j\}) - f(Y).

In plain language, this says that the marginal value of adding jj to XX is greater than when adding it to YY. Thus, submodularity expresses a form of diminishing marginal returns (for set functions) that is analogous to concavity (for continuous functions).

Submodular functions have a number of desirable traits. A famous result of Nemhauser et al. (1978) holds that when maximizing a monotone1 submodular function over a cardinality constraint, the greedy algorithm that iteratively adds to XX the element that yields the greatest increase in f(X)f(X) is 1−1e≈63.2%1 - \frac{1}{e} \approx 63.2\% optimal.2 Efficient algorithms that provide the same approximation ratio for more complex constraint structures have also been identified (Badanidiyuru et al. 2014, Chekuri et al. 2014, Kulik et al. 2013). See Vondrák (2017) for an introduction to submodular functions with many examples.

Preliminary interpretation of f(X)f(X)

The function f(X)f(X) defined above can be used to model the probability of a parallel system failing when optional repairs are made to its components. For example, suppose there are a dozen roads between our town and the next, and the probabilities of each road being open in the coming winter are given by p1p_1 through p12p_{12}. For each of the roads, we can engage in repairs to increase the availability to qiq_i. Letting XX denote the set of roads that we repair, the probability of at least one road being open in the winter is one minus the probability that all of them are closed, which is f(X).f(X).

With this interpretation in mind, we are tempted to give f(X)f(X) a tortured-but-accurate name such as the “parallel process success probability with quality upgrades” function—but we’ll just stick with f(X)f(X) for now.

The applicability of f(X)f(X) in its current form is, admittedly, a bit narrow. The versatility of this functional form shows itself more clearly when we start taking conic combinations—see the examples below.

Monotonicity and submodularity proof

Theorem: f(X)f(X) as defined above is a monotone submodular function.

Proof: The monotonicity of f(X)f(X) is clear from the constraints on pp and qq: Adding any element jj to XX replaces the 1−pj1 - p_j term with the (smaller) 1−qj1 - q_j term, increasing the overall function value.

To prove submodularity, pick any X⊆Y⊆ΩX \subseteq Y \subseteq \Omega and j∈Ω∖Y.j \in \Omega \setminus Y. If pj<1,p_j < 1, we have

f(X∪{j})−f(X)=[1−1−qj1−pj∏i∈Ω∖X(1−pi)∏i∈X(1−qi)]−[1−∏i∈Ω∖X(1−pi)∏i∈X(1−qi)]=−(1−qj1−pj)∏i∈Ω∖X(1−pi)∏i∈X(1−qi)+∏i∈Ω∖X(1−pi)∏i∈X(1−qi)=(1−1−qj1−pj)(1−f(X))≥(1−1−qj1−pj)(1−f(Y))=f(Y∪{j})−f(Y). \begin{aligned} f(X \cup \{j\}) - f(X) &= \biggl[1 - \frac{1 - q_j}{1 - p_j} \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} (1 - p_i) \prod_{i \in X} (1 - q_i) \biggr] - \biggl[1 - \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} (1 - p_i) \prod_{i \in X} (1 - q_i) \biggr] \\ &= - \biggl(\frac{1 - q_j}{1 - p_j}\biggr) \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} (1 - p_i) \prod_{i \in X} (1 - q_i) + \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} (1 - p_i) \prod_{i \in X} (1 - q_i) \\ &= \biggl(1 - \frac{1 - q_j}{1 - p_j}\biggr) \Bigl(1 - f(X)\Bigr) \\ &\geq \biggl(1 - \frac{1 - q_j}{1 - p_j}\biggr) \Bigl(1 - f(Y)\Bigr) \\ &= f(Y \cup \{j\}) - f(Y). \end{aligned}

The penultimate inequality follows from monotonicity, and the final equality is just applying the first three steps in reverse, with YY instead of X.X.

In the pj=1p_j = 1 case, f(X∪{j})=1f(X \cup \{j\}) = 1 for any XX and the inequality collapses to −f(X)≥−f(Y),- f(X) \geq - f(Y), which is just monotonicity. ◼

Application: Optimal scrum velocity, aka the mm-dimensional Zeno walk

Let’s examine a plausible(ish) application of this functional form.

The Zeno walk: Consider a Zeno walk between an arbitrary origin and destination. Let Ω\Omega provide a set of possible steps that we can take toward our destination. Each step’s size is si∈[0,1],s_i \in [0, 1], which means that if you choose to take step i,i, you get 100si100 s_i percent of the way from your current location to the destination.3 If XX is the set of steps you choose to take, you will make it

g(X)=1−∏i∈X(1−si) g(X) = 1 - \prod_{i \in X} (1 - s_i)

of the way to the destination from your starting point. This g(X)g(X) is just f(X)f(X) where each pi=0p_i = 0 and qi=siq_i = s_i.

The multidimensional Zeno walk: Now, consider a process optimization problem. We have a set Ψ\Psi (indexed by jj) of goals to achieve, as well as a set Ω\Omega (indexed by ii) of tasks we can complete in service of the goals. Tasks can advance multiple goals at once. Let vijv_{ij} denote the effectiveness of task ii against goal j,j, meaning that if you engage in task i,i, then vijv_{ij} of (whatever remains of)4 goal jj will be completed. Each goal is worth tj≥0t_j \geq 0 story points, and goals can be completed fractionally.

We would like to choose the set of tasks XX that maximizes the team’s velocity in the current planning period, defined as the total number of story points completed, which is

h(X)=∑j∈Ψ(tj−tj∏i∈X(1−vij)). h(X) = \sum_{j \in \Psi} \Bigl( t_j - t_j \prod_{i \in X} (1 - v_{ij}) \Bigr).

Tasks are subject to a variety of constraints due to considerations such as cost, scheduling, and dependency. It is not immediately obvious which tasks we should prioritize—for example, should we favor tasks that advance many different goals, or those that have high effectiveness against the most important goals? Well, the jjth term of h(X)h(X) is just tjg(X)t_j g(X) where ss is the jjth column of vv. Thus, h(X),h(X), as a conic combination of submodular functions, is itself submodular,5 and we can compute the optimal velocity using any of the well-known techniques for submodular maximization.

Returning to Zeno’s paradox, the business process described above is akin to a multidimensional Zeno walk in which tjt_j gives your distance from the target in the jjth coordinate axis, and each step ii moves you vijv_{ij} of the way towards the target along that axis. The function h(X)h(X) measures the one-norm distance between your final position and the target.

Remarks

Linearization: To maximize f(X)f(X) as written, in typical cases, it will be simplest to minimize 1−f(X),1 - f(X), which is a modular function after taking the logarithm.6 The logarithm trick doesn’t work, however, for conic combinations of f(X)f(X); the best we can do is introduce helper variables and reformulate the problem as an integer convex program.7 Thus, recognizing the submodularity of f(X)f(X) (and the strong approximation results implied) is worthwhile.

Serial processes: It is not difficult to show, using a similar proof as above, that the “serial process success probability with quality upgrades” function

f~(X)=∏i∈Ω∖Xpi∏i∈Xqi \tilde f(X) = \prod_{i \in \Omega \setminus X} p_i \prod_{i \in X} q_i

with pi≤qip_i \leq q_i is monotone and supermodular; that is, −f~(X)- \tilde f(X) is monotone decreasing and submodular. Note that 1−f~(X)1 - \tilde f(X) has the same functional form as the original f(X),f(X), but the inequality on pip_i and qiq_i comes out backwards. This turns back into the original result (that f(X)f(X) is increasing and submodular) if you interchange the roles of XX and Ω∖X\Omega \setminus X.
